# Brand UI

Brand UI covers marketing sites, landing pages, launch pages, portfolio pages, editorial/content surfaces, campaign pages, and other screens where the visitor's impression is the deliverable.

Design creates memory here. A safe page can be technically correct and still fail because nobody remembers it.

## Brand UI principles

- Pick a point of view before styling. Name the audience, the mood, and the physical or cultural reference behind the surface.
- Distinctiveness matters more than conformity, but distinctiveness must still serve the message.
- Commit to a color strategy: restrained, committed, full palette, or drenched. Do not hover halfway between them.
- Typography can carry the brand, but do not reach for the same fashionable display faces by reflex.
- Imagery matters when the category implies it: restaurants, hotels, travel, fashion, photography, products, artists, and communities usually need real imagery or credible generated/illustrated assets.
- Layout can be asymmetric, editorial, gridded, maximal, minimal, dense, or quiet. The failure is a default centered stack with repeated feature cards.
- Motion can be more expressive than product UI, but it should support pacing and story, not distract from content.

## Direction before build

For new or ambiguous brand surfaces, answer these before coding:

- What should the visitor remember?
- Who is this for, and what do they already believe?
- What should it explicitly not look like?
- What is the visual reference? Use concrete references, not adjectives like modern, clean, premium.
- What carries the identity: type, color, imagery, layout, motion, copy voice, or a signature motif?
- What real content and assets are available? Do not ship fake placeholder sections as if they are finished.

## Typography

- Choose fonts from the brand voice, not from current AI-design fashion.
- A single strong family can be better than a weak display/body pairing.
- Use clear scale contrast for brand moments. Flat type scales read timid.
- Keep body copy readable: appropriate line length, line-height, and contrast.
- Reserve uppercase tracking for short labels. Repeating tiny uppercase kickers before every section often reads like scaffolding.

## Color

- Restrained: mostly neutral, one rare accent. Works for quiet confidence.
- Committed: one color carries a large part of the surface. Works for strong identity.
- Full palette: several named roles used deliberately. Works for campaigns, expressive brands, data-rich storytelling.
- Drenched: the surface is the color. Works when the brand moment needs intensity.

Do not default to purple/blue gradients, neon-on-black, or beige editorial minimalism unless the actual brand demands it.

## Layout

- Avoid the generic hero stack: eyebrow chip, huge centered headline, subtitle, two buttons, three metrics/cards.
- Use space as rhythm: tight related groups, generous section breaks, deliberate asymmetry or a strict visible grid.
- Cards are not the default. Use them only when items need comparison, separation, or direct action.
- One decisive visual idea beats five decorative flourishes.

## Motion and delight

- Page-load or scroll motion can work on brand surfaces, but one well-composed moment is better than constant movement.
- Keep motion performant, interruptible, and reduced-motion aware.
- Delight should fit the audience. Do not add playful effects to a serious brand without reason.

## Common brand UI failures

- A surface that could belong to any startup after swapping the logo.
- Centered everything, identical cards, rounded icon tiles, and generic gradient accents.
- No real imagery where imagery is the point.
- Type chosen for trendiness rather than voice.
- Copy that announces quality instead of demonstrating it.
