# E-E-A-T Framework

**E**xperience, **E**xpertise, **A**uthoritativeness, **T**rustworthiness. Google's Quality Rater Guidelines (QRG) framework, updated September 2025. After the December 2025 core update, E-E-A-T applies to **all competitive queries** (not just YMYL).

## Critical: December 2025 core update

The December 2025 core update was described as a "watershed moment" that:
- Extended E-E-A-T evaluation to virtually all competitive queries
- Made author attribution standards tighter across all categories
- Penalized anonymous or generic authorship even for non-YMYL content
- Significantly improved AI content quality detection

**Impact by industry:**
| Industry | Average traffic decline |
|---|---|
| Affiliate sites | 71% |
| Health / YMYL | 67% |
| E-commerce | 52% |

**Bottom line:** Even entertainment and lifestyle content now requires demonstrated expertise. Generic content no longer ranks competitively.

## YMYL (Your Money or Your Life)

Topics requiring the **highest** E-E-A-T standards. After Dec 2025, E-E-A-T matters everywhere, but YMYL bar is still highest:

- Health and safety
- Financial advice and transactions
- Legal information
- News and current events
- **Elections and civic trust** (added Sept 2025)
- **Democratic processes** (added Sept 2025)
- Groups of people (potential for harm)

## The four dimensions

### Experience (weight: 20%)

First-hand knowledge and personal involvement with the topic.

#### Signals to check
- [ ] Author has demonstrable first-hand experience with the topic
- [ ] Content includes original photos, screenshots, or data
- [ ] Case studies or real-world examples with specific details
- [ ] Personal process documentation or methodology descriptions
- [ ] Before/after results or outcome data
- [ ] Specific anecdotes that couldn't be fabricated

#### Scoring
- **Strong:** Multiple first-hand experience signals, original content
- **Moderate:** Some personal experience evident
- **Weak:** Generic information, no personal touch
- **None:** Clearly AI-generated or scraped content

#### Examples

**Strong experience:**
> "When I tested 12 vocal compressors over the past year on my own recordings, the FabFilter Pro-C 2 was the only one that handled sibilants on female vocals without artifacts. Here's the exact settings I use [screenshot] and the audio comparison [embedded clip]."

**Weak experience:**
> "Vocal compressors are an important part of any mixing engineer's toolkit. There are many great options on the market, including the FabFilter Pro-C 2, which many engineers consider to be one of the best."

The first sounds like someone who actually did the work. The second sounds like someone who looked it up.

### Expertise (weight: 25%)

Formal qualifications, training, and demonstrated knowledge.

#### Signals to check
- [ ] Author credentials relevant to topic (bio, certifications)
- [ ] Technical accuracy and depth appropriate for audience
- [ ] Claims supported by evidence or sources
- [ ] Specialized vocabulary used correctly
- [ ] Up-to-date with current developments in the field
- [ ] Byline with author name and credentials visible

#### Scoring
- **Strong:** Verified credentials, deep technical accuracy, accurate use of jargon
- **Moderate:** Demonstrable knowledge, some credentials
- **Weak:** Surface-level information, no credentials
- **None:** Factual errors, misinformation, generic content

#### How to demonstrate
- Author bio with relevant credentials (degrees, certifications, years of experience, notable work)
- Person schema with `jobTitle`, `worksFor`, `alumniOf`
- Citations to primary sources (research papers, official docs, government data)
- Technical accuracy you can verify
- Correct use of specialized terms

### Authoritativeness (weight: 25%)

Recognition by others as a go-to source.

#### Signals to check
- [ ] Site recognized as authority in its niche
- [ ] Author recognized as expert (external citations, speaking, publications)
- [ ] Content cited by other authoritative sources
- [ ] Industry awards, certifications, or accreditations
- [ ] Consistent publication history in the topic area
- [ ] Featured in reputable media outlets
- [ ] Professional affiliations

#### Scoring
- **Strong:** Widely recognized authority, cited by others, media coverage
- **Moderate:** Growing recognition, some external validation
- **Weak:** No external recognition
- **None:** Negative reputation, known for misinformation

#### How to build it
- **Earned Wikipedia/Wikidata presence** for the brand and key people (only when notability standards are genuinely met)
- **Industry publications** featuring you or quoting you
- **Speaking engagements** at conferences
- **Awards** in your space
- **Backlinks** from authoritative sites in your niche
- **Co-citations** — being mentioned alongside other recognized experts
- **SameAs** schema linking to your professional profiles

### Trustworthiness (weight: 30%)

The most important factor. Overall reliability and transparency.

#### Signals to check
- [ ] Clear contact information (physical address, phone, email)
- [ ] Privacy policy and terms of service
- [ ] HTTPS with valid certificate
- [ ] Transparent about who creates content and why
- [ ] Customer reviews and testimonials
- [ ] Corrections and update history visible
- [ ] No deceptive practices (hidden ads, clickbait)
- [ ] Secure payment processing (for e-commerce)
- [ ] Return/refund policy visible
- [ ] About page with real information about the company

#### Scoring
- **Strong:** Full transparency, verified business, positive reputation
- **Moderate:** Good trust signals, minor gaps
- **Weak:** Missing key trust signals
- **None:** Deceptive practices, scam indicators

#### Trust killers
- Hiding the company behind opaque branding
- No physical address or contact
- Anonymous authorship
- No author bios
- No date on content
- Excessive ads or popups
- Affiliate links not disclosed
- Pricing hidden behind "contact sales"
- Fake reviews, testimonials, or community astroturfing
- Fake author identities, invented credentials, or deceptive bylines
- Negative reviews on third-party sites (G2, Capterra, Trustpilot)
- News coverage about deceptive practices

## September 2025 QRG updates

### AI content assessment

Raters now formally evaluate whether content appears AI-generated:
- AI content is **acceptable** if it demonstrates genuine E-E-A-T
- Low-quality AI content (generic, no unique value) is penalized
- The presence of AI-generated content is not inherently penalizing
- What matters: does the content provide unique value regardless of creation method?

### Markers of low-quality AI content (per QRG)
- Generic phrasing without specificity
- Lack of original insight or unique perspective
- No first-hand experience signals
- Factual inaccuracies
- Repetitive structure across multiple pages
- No author attribution or expertise signals

### New spam categories
- **Expired domain abuse** — Buying expired domains for their backlinks and repurposing
- **Site reputation abuse** — Using a reputable site to host low-quality content (e.g., a major newspaper hosting AI-generated coupon pages)
- **Scaled content abuse** — Mass-producing content without value (programmatic SEO done badly)

### AI Overview evaluation
Raters now assess the quality of AI-generated summaries in search results, providing feedback that affects future model training.

### RSL 1.0 (Really Simple Licensing)
New machine-readable content licensing standard (December 2025) for AI training:
- Backed by Reddit, Yahoo, Medium, Quora, Cloudflare, Akamai, Creative Commons
- Allows publishers to specify AI licensing terms
- Augments robots.txt for AI-specific permissions

## Experience signals are critical differentiators

The December 2025 update elevated the "Experience" dimension as a key differentiator:

- First-person narrative ("I tested this...", "In my experience...")
- Original photos and screenshots (not stock images)
- Specific examples with verifiable details
- Process documentation showing actual work done
- Original data, surveys, or research

**Why:** AI can generate expertise-sounding content but cannot fabricate genuine experience. Google has explicitly leaned into this as the differentiator that AI can't fake.

## Overall scoring guide

| Score | Description |
|---|---|
| 90-100 | Exceptional E-E-A-T, authority site, recognized expert, full transparency |
| 70-89 | Strong E-E-A-T, demonstrated expertise, good trust signals |
| 50-69 | Moderate E-E-A-T, some signals, room for improvement |
| 30-49 | Weak E-E-A-T, minimal signals, significant gaps |
| 0-29 | Very low E-E-A-T, no visible signals, potential trust issues |

## Improvement recommendations by score

### 0-29 (Critical)
1. Add contact information and about page
2. Establish author identity with credentials
3. Implement HTTPS
4. Remove deceptive elements
5. Add real company information

### 30-49 (Major)
1. Add author bios with credentials
2. Include first-hand experience content
3. Get external citations / mentions
4. Add customer testimonials
5. Add publication and update dates

### 50-69 (Moderate)
1. Deepen content with original research
2. Build topical authority through content clusters
3. Pursue industry recognition
4. Document processes and methodologies
5. Add Person and ProfilePage schema for authors

### 70-89 (Minor)
1. Maintain freshness with regular updates
2. Expand author presence across platforms
3. Pursue speaking / publication opportunities
4. Add video / multimedia demonstrating expertise
5. Build entity presence (Wikipedia, Wikidata)

### 90-100 (Maintenance)
1. Continue publishing high-quality content
2. Monitor and respond to reputation issues
3. Keep credentials and certifications current
4. Refresh top content quarterly

## Implementation checklist

For any content site or blog targeting competitive queries:

### Author setup
- [ ] Every post has a visible byline with author name
- [ ] Author name links to an author page
- [ ] Author page has bio with relevant credentials
- [ ] Author page has Person schema with `sameAs` to LinkedIn, Twitter, etc.
- [ ] Author page uses ProfilePage schema (added 2025)

### Site-wide trust
- [ ] About page with real company info, founders, team
- [ ] Contact page with multiple contact methods
- [ ] Privacy policy
- [ ] Terms of service
- [ ] HTTPS everywhere
- [ ] Footer has company name, address, year

### Per-post quality
- [ ] First-person experience signals where possible
- [ ] Original photos / screenshots (not stock)
- [ ] Specific data with sources and dates
- [ ] Citations to authoritative sources
- [ ] Last-updated date visible
- [ ] Article schema with `author`, `datePublished`, `dateModified`

### External signals
- [ ] Wikipedia/Wikidata presence for brand only if independently notable and guideline-compliant
- [ ] LinkedIn company page complete
- [ ] Industry publication coverage
- [ ] Genuine third-party reviews on G2, Trustpilot, etc.
- [ ] Speaking or podcast appearances

## Common E-E-A-T mistakes

- **Anonymous content** — no byline, no "by [name]"
- **Generic author bios** — "John is a writer who loves technology"
- **No credentials shown** — author has expertise but doesn't display it
- **Stock photos as "experience"** — readers spot this immediately
- **Vague claims** — "many users", "industry leaders", "studies show"
- **Outdated content** — content from 2022 with no update for competitive queries
- **Over-claiming expertise** — bio claims credentials the author doesn't have
- **Missing About page** — site looks faceless
- **Hidden ownership** — readers can't tell who runs the site
- **Affiliate links not disclosed** — undisclosed financial interest is a major trust killer
- **Plagiarism** — content lifted from other sources is the worst trust signal
